# A1: our own mind architecture, design round 1

Date: 2026-09-25. Every design here is **[H]**: a hypothesis, not tested. It must replace the C1 child-mind, a standard small Transformer (`plan.md` §1.3, D4), by beating it on the scoreboard at equal size.

## The job

C1's job and A1's: **read a handful of facts and a question; answer from the facts, or say "unknown".** Later, Design D supplies the facts.

## Constraints from the evidence

| # | Constraint | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| K1 | Must do associative recall from context: find the one fact that answers | Pure recurrent and convolutional models lose mainly on recall: "82% of the gap is explained by … associative recall" (Zoology); pure Mamba fails phonebook lookup "beyond approximately 500 tokens" (NVIDIA) [C, survey §4.4] |
| K2 | Cheap on a 4-core CPU | Token generation is bound by memory bandwidth (~20 GB/s here, provisional) [V/S, survey §2, §4.4] |
| K3 | "Unknown" must be a built-in outcome, not guessed from token probabilities | Token confidence predicts failure near chance (53.83 AUROC on a robot model) [C✓]; confident near-misses [C✓] |
| K4 | Must not be the Transformer: no stack of token-to-token self-attention blocks as the core | The project's end goal, `plan.md` §1.1 Rule 2 |
| K5 | Equal-size, equal-data comparison against C1 | `plan.md` §8 rule 9 |

## Design α: fact-level reader with a "none" slot

**Idea.** Humans do not re-read every word against every other word. They read each fact once, then ask "which fact answers this?" So:
1. **Read each fact once** with a small, cheap encoder (a short recurrent or convolutional pass over that fact's bytes only), giving one vector per fact.
2. **Read the question** the same way.
3. **Choose among the facts, plus one learned "none of these" slot.** The question scores each fact vector and the none-slot; a softmax picks one.
4. **Answer by copying** the value span from the chosen fact (a pointer over its bytes), or output "unknown" if the none-slot wins.

**Why it might beat the Transformer**
- K1: choosing among facts is exactly the recall operation, done once per fact rather than across every pair of tokens.
- K2: cost scales with (number of facts × one small pass), not (tokens²) or (tokens × layers × model size) per generated byte. Answers are copied, not generated byte by byte [H: arithmetic needed].
- K3: "none" is a first-class choice with its own probability, the same idea as Design D's "none of these" option, now inside the mind.
- K4: no token-to-token self-attention stack. One attention step over fact vectors.

**Known risks**
- **Prior art likely:** memory networks (end-to-end memory networks attend over sentence vectors), pointer networks and pointer-sentinel models (a learned "sentinel" choice beside the pointer) [R, needs a search]. Novelty would have to come from the combination, calibrated "none", and CPU cost.
- **Encoder weakness:** "cat" vs "dog" must survive the one-vector-per-fact squeeze. A mean-pooled fact vector could blur them.
- **Only fits fact-shaped input.** Stories and multi-step reasoning need more.

## Design β: recurrent core with a fast associative scratchpad

**Idea.** A linear-time recurrent reader (cheap on CPU, constant memory per step), plus a small "scratchpad" matrix it writes key→value associations into as it reads, updated by a correction rule: store only what the scratchpad did not already predict. At the question, it reads back by key. If the read-back matches poorly (a large residual between query and best stored key), that is the "unknown" signal.

**Why it might beat the Transformer**
- K2: fixed-size state; each byte costs the same regardless of context length.
- K1: the scratchpad targets exactly the recall weakness of pure recurrent models.
- K3: the read-back residual is a mechanical "not found" signal.

**Known risks**
- **Heavy prior art [R]:** fast-weight programmers, DeltaNet / gated DeltaNet, Titans all use delta-rule associative state. Our twist (a residual-based "unknown") must be checked.
- Scratchpad capacity is fixed; many similar facts may collide (the cat/dog case again).
- Training recurrent models in fp16 on a T4 has known instability (red-team note in plan v2).

## Design γ: bound-vector (holographic) memory reader

**Idea.** Encode each fact as a *binding* of three vectors, subject ⊛ relation ⊛ value, superposed into one memory vector (vector-symbolic architectures). A question binds subject ⊛ relation and *unbinds* the memory, giving a noisy value vector. A clean-up step matches it to known values; a poor match means "unknown".

**Why it might beat the Transformer**
- K2: extremely cheap (a few vector operations).
- K3: the clean-up similarity is a natural "unknown" score.
- It is the most brain-like and the most different from the Transformer.

**Known risks**
- The survey found no generative language model built this way; the approach was "largely overshadowed" by learned embeddings [C, survey §4.2].
- It needs the question parsed into subject and relation first, which is the hard part.
- Superposition noise grows with the number of facts.

## Proposed race (kill test, to finalise after red team)

- **Same task and data** as C1: training worlds 1000+, validation worlds 900+, and the scoreboard on seed 0.
- **Equal parameters** (about 4.9M) and equal training steps and batch size.
- **Metrics:** in-context answer EM, "unknown" accuracy, held-out question phrasing EM, "unknown" AUROC, CPU ms per answer on this laptop.
- **A candidate replaces C1 only if** it is no worse than C1 on EM and unknown accuracy (within a pre-set margin), and better on held-out phrasing EM or on CPU cost (by a pre-set margin, CI excluding zero, 5 seeds).
- **Kill** a candidate that is worse on EM or unknown accuracy beyond the margin, whatever its speed.

## Red-team verdict (2026-09-25)

An adversarial review read 21 papers and ran two small scripts. I re-ran its most important check myself.

### The race track is broken (fix before any architecture race)
- **A 30-line regex built from the generator's own templates scores answer EM 1.000 and "unknown" 1.000** on 1,000 validation examples (worlds 900–904) [V, re-run by hand; the same check now lives in `scoreboard/regex_reader.py`]. C1 scored 0.906 / 0.915 on a task pattern-matching solves. The in-distribution task measures template parsing, not reading.
- **The same regex scores 0.000 on held-out question phrasings** [V]. Only the held-out test measures understanding, and it has **12 templates** (one per relation).
- The probe's keyword variant (0.905 held-out EM) is **not a fair baseline**: its keyword table contains words from the held-out templates ("commute", "profession"). That is leakage, found on re-running the check, not in the review.
- `childmind/data.py` never uses the held-out *fact* phrasings the world already generates [V].
- C1 inference re-runs the whole prompt for every answer byte (no KV cache, `childmind/infer.py:17`) [V]. This inflates C1's CPU cost, so CPU comparisons against it are biased in the candidates' favour.

### Architecture verdicts
| Design | Verdict | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|
| α fact reader + "none" slot + pointer | **Already done** as a combination: key-value memory networks key facts by subject + relation [C]; Levy et al. 2017 add "a trainable bias b … essentially setting and learning a threshold" for no-answer [C]. Becomes a **strong baseline**, not an invention. | arXiv 1606.03126, 1706.04115 |
| β recurrent + delta-rule scratchpad | **Architecture already done**: Fast Weight Memory (2020) reached 96.75% on catbAbI QA with 694k weights against 87.66% for a 10.5M Transformer-XL [C]. **Read-back residual as an "unknown" signal: appears open** (two searches, not exhaustive). The residual stops working once writes reach the key size per head [V toy simulation: residual 0.000 for absent keys at 32 stored keys with 32 dimensions], so writes must be gated. | arXiv 2011.07831 |
| γ holographic binding | **Core done in 2018** (TPR-RNN, bAbI 10k error 1.34) [C]. Capacity is limiting: with perfect parsing at d=256, recall falls from 0.953 (8 facts) to 0.757 (16 facts) [V toy simulation]. The byte-to-triple parser is the hard part. | arXiv 1811.12143 |

### What survives as a possible invention
A fast-weight reader (FWM-style) with **gated writes** (a cap on writes per fact) and a **separate key memory whose read-back residual drives "unknown"**, reading the question first or reading twice (JRT). This is narrow, and it rests on a limited novelty search.

### Required before any A1 race (scoreboard v2)
1. Harder world:
   - many more question *and* fact phrasings, with held-out sets of real size;
   - two-hop questions (a sister's city);
   - updates ("moved to");
   - negation;
   - same-name distractors;
   - more test worlds.
2. Baselines as floors:
   - the regex solver;
   - a bag-of-words key-value memory network (the 2016 method).
3. Fair CPU comparison:
   - give C1 a KV cache;
   - add a copy-constrained C1;
   - report FLOPs and milliseconds;
   - require parameters and compute within ±10%.
4. Train every entrant on the answer only, with no extra per-fact supervision unless all entrants get it.
5. Keep one prompt order for all entrants.

## A1 candidate 1: "Fast-weight memory reader" (FWMR), pre-registered 2026-09-25

**Written before any code or training.** All claims [H] until raced.

### Mechanism
1. **Encode each fact once, on its own.** A small byte-level bidirectional recurrent encoder (GRU) turns each fact into a key vector `k` (who + what), a value vector `v`, and per-byte states used for copying.
2. **Write into a fast-weight memory, in order, with the delta rule:** `M += β (v − M k) kᵀ`. It stores only what the memory did not already predict. A later fact with the same key overwrites the earlier value, which is how "Priya now lives in Pune" replaces "Priya lives in Chennai". A matching key memory `P += β (k − P k) kᵀ` learns the span of stored keys.
3. **Read at question time.** Encode the question into a query `q`. Read `r = M q`. Up to 2 read steps, where step 2 forms a new query from step 1's result, for "Priya's sister's city".
4. **"I don't know" from geometry, not token probabilities:** the read-back residual `‖q − P q‖` (how far the query lies from anything stored) plus the read vector feed a small unknown head. Negated facts ("has no dog") are written like facts and must read back as "unknown".
5. **Answer by copying** a span from the facts: start and end pointers over the fact bytes, weighted by which stored value the read matches. No byte-by-byte generation.
6. **Rationed writes:** one write per fact, not per byte. With at most 8 facts per context and key size ≥ 64, the residual stays informative, per the red team's toy simulation.

### Why it might beat the Transformer
- **Cost:** facts are encoded once when written. A question costs one question encoding plus a memory read, **independent of how many facts are stored**. C1 must re-read every fact for every question unless given prefix caching.
- **Updates and honesty** come from the mechanism (overwrite, residual), not from learned token statistics.

### Honest prior-art position
GRU encoders, delta-rule fast weights (Fast Weight Memory, 2020), multi-hop reads (memory networks) and pointer copying are all published. **Not ours.** What is claimed as new:
- the key-projection residual as the "unknown" signal, with rationed per-fact writes;
- measured calibrated abstention on near-misses, wrong-person traps, updates and negation;
- per-question CPU cost independent of stored facts.

The novelty search was limited (red team: two searches).

### Fairness (from the red team)
- **Same training data** as the opponent: `scoreboard.reading`, training worlds 1000+, training categories only (no held-out wording).
- **Same supervision:** answer string only. Span positions come from finding the answer string in the facts; no label says which fact is correct.
- **Parameters within ±10%** of C1: 4.9M.
- **The opponent is C1 retrained on the same v2 data.** Frozen architecture, same step budget.
- **Test:** worlds 800–804 through `scoreboard/read_race.py`, all 9 categories.

### Pass/fail (pre-registered)
- **Replaces C1 only if both hold:**
  - (a) Overall answerable EM and unknown accuracy are each no more than 0.02 below C1-v2.
  - (b) Either (i) mean accuracy over the three held-out categories is at least **0.05 higher**, with the 95% CI over 5 seeds excluding 0, or (ii) CPU ms per question with facts pre-encoded is at least **2× lower** at (a)-level accuracy.
- **Killed** if (a) fails.
- **The honesty claim stands only if** its AUROC over ALL beats C1-v2's with the CI excluding 0.
- **One-seed runs are smoke tests, not results.**

**Amended 2026-09-26**, after the seed-0 smoke run on the validation worlds and before any run on the test worlds. Both changes fix a definition and make the test harder to pass; no threshold moves.
1. **AUROC labels.** A prediction is positive only when it is a correct real answer, as in the memory scoreboard.
   - Every entrant's confidence means "I hold a real answer", but the old label counted a correct "unknown" as positive.
   - Correct abstentions therefore scored as confident errors: both minds came out near 0.38 in the smoke run.
   - `read_race` now enforces the new label with a self-check: the all-templates reader must score exactly 1.000.
2. **(b)(i) uses answerable EM on the three held-out categories, not accuracy.**
   - About half of those questions are "unknown", so a reader can raise their accuracy by refusing more.
   - In the smoke run, the train-template regex, which reads no held-out wording, averaged 0.495 there. A1 averaged 0.488, mostly from refusals: answerable EM 0.056, 0.030 and 0.017.
   - The margin stays at 0.05, with the 95% CI over 5 seeds excluding 0.
